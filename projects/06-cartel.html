01Context & Objective

The problem isn’t the cartel. It’s the coordination.

A supply chain under pressure is one of the hardest things to imitate in software, because nothing in it happens in order. Raw material is produced while last week’s product is still shipping; a lab breaks down as a courier chooses a route; money leaves the books on a schedule that cares nothing for whether anything was sold. Every part moves at once, and the only thing holding it together is how carefully the parts are allowed to touch.

This project models exactly that, using a deliberately adversarial subject — a fictional narcotics operation, chosen because it packs every hard coordination problem into one system: production, processing, logistics, a hostile environment, a workforce whose morale feeds back into its own output, and a bank balance that can end the whole thing. It is an abstract stress test for concurrent design, not an account of anything real; the tokens moving through the queue could be anything. What follows is a tour of the machine — its wiring, its patterns, and the parameter space it lives in — before we ever press start.

02The Model

Sixty-odd minds, one ledger, no manager.

The simulation runs 36+ concurrent threads — 20 farmers, 10 chemists, 3 labs, a courier fleet, plus standalone threads for payroll and for the collective morale of the workforce. None of them calls another directly. They meet only at shared resources, and every meeting point is guarded by exactly the right synchronisation primitive. The whole system is that topology of hand-offs:

In plain terms Instead of one narrator describing events one at a time, every worker gets its own independent brain running at the same moment as everyone else’s. Money is one shared jar of cash many hands reach into — a Lock is the rule that says “one hand at a time,” the difference between correct books and quietly corrupted ones.

Why a bounded queue, a condition, and a lock — three different tools?

Each guards a different kind of hand-off. The bounded queue lets fast farmers and slow chemists run at their own pace without the fast side burying the slow side — producers block when it’s full, consumers block when it’s empty. The condition is a wait/notify handshake: couriers sleep until enough product exists, and a chemist wakes them the instant it does. The lock protects a single shared number (the funds) from being mangled when two threads touch it at once. Right tool, right hand-off.

The four patterns that shape behaviour

Underneath the topology, behaviour is organised through four classical design patterns — each a small, reusable answer to “how should this part be allowed to change?” The shapes themselves tell the story:

State
The Lab

Running / Maintenance / Compromised. The facility’s behaviour is its state, not a thicket of if-statements — flip the state, flip the rules.

Observer
Morale

One signal, many listeners. A single morale value broadcasts to every worker; each recalculates its own efficiency the instant morale moves.

Chain of Responsibility
Quality → Storage

Each link handles its bit or passes it on. A leaf is checked, then filed — and any link can drop it out of the chain.

Strategy
Route choice

Swap the rule, keep the courier. One interchangeable slot holds the route-picking policy — chase revenue, avoid risk, or balance the two.

The parameter space, part one — the routes

Distribution is where the model gets to be interesting on paper. Twelve real routes span three transport types, each trading revenue against seizure risk. But the risk is cheap: a caught shipment is bribed through 80% of the time, so the true expected revenue per unit is only revenue × (1 − 0.6 · risk). Toggle the chart to price that in — the points barely fall, which quietly rewrites which strategy is smart.

The parameter space, part two — morale is the master variable

The single most consequential number in the system is morale, because it enters the model twice. It sets each worker’s efficiency (how fast their loop turns) and it bends every failure probability (crop failures, raids). Combine the model’s real probabilities and you can plot morale’s effect before running anything at all: the yield per leaf sags only gently, but total throughput collapses, because low morale slows the loops and raises the failure rates at the same time.

Everything is written down

Nothing lives only in memory. Every event — a harvest, a rejection, a seizure, a morale hit — is timestamped, tagged with a run ID and written to a normalised SQLite database of eight tables, each write on its own short-lived connection (the correct way to reach SQLite from many threads). That ledger is what makes the run legible afterwards: a companion module reads it back with pandas and seaborn — success rates, rolling averages, confidence intervals, a morale-to-production correlation — recovering the emergent story a leaderless system won’t narrate while it runs.

An honest caveat about the word “parallel”

Under CPython’s global interpreter lock these threads run concurrently (interleaved, with real synchronisation problems) rather than in CPU parallel. For a simulation dominated by waiting and sleeping that is the right model — the hard parts (races, starvation, deadlock) are identical to the truly-parallel case, which is exactly what the project set out to practise. Other honest limits: the probabilities are hand-tuned, not empirically grounded, and the code leans on blanket “catch-and-continue” error handling that can let failures pass in silence — a trait the example run below puts on display.

03Run it once

What one run actually does.

The model above is the design; a run is one roll of its dice. Here is a single representative run, offered as illustration rather than verdict — the numbers shift from run to run, but the shape recurs. The front of the pipeline springs to life: 138 leaves harvested, 87 waved through the quality gate. And then the chain flatlines — nothing survives the hand-off into storage, so processing and distribution never fire.

Meanwhile the morale loop — run at a high chaotic factor — does exactly what Fig. 3 predicts it would. Desertions, paranoia and sabotage ratchet it downward with no recovery mechanic, and in this run it crosses the 0.10 threshold and aborts the simulation early.

The through-line

Most of this project is a machine you can inspect before it moves: a topology of synchronised hand-offs, four patterns doing four honest jobs, and a parameter space you can chart on paper — routes whose risk is cheaper than it looks, and a morale variable that governs the whole throughput curve.

Press start and the model keeps its promises. The routes go unused, the morale curve bends exactly as Fig. 3 says it must, and the ledger fills with a faithful account of a system quietly seizing up. The design is the point; the run is the proof that the design behaves.
